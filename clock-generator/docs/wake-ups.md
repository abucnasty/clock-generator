# Wake-ups and wake-up lists

This page was split out of GOALS.md on 2026-10-15; GOALS.md holds the goals.

## How clocking impacts updates per second
Clocking can reduce updates per second in theory by minimizing active inserter windows. Most notably, minimize the amount of crafts an inserter sees on its source or sink while idle and not transferring materials. These count as wasted updated checks. This doesn't always translate to improved performance on all hardware, but for the intents and purposes of this algorithm, it is assumed that it *always* does. The choice of removing a clock from an inserter is left up to the user and they can do this by overriding the inserter to "always" enabled in the config. An inserter that is set to "always" enabled is to get no combinator in the blueprint.

Given a recipe chain to produce a target end product, the amount of items that are needed are consistent in this build. Mathematically, the amount of items that need to be transferred is an independent concern of clocking. What clocking can control however is *when* those items get transferred.

Inserters have been measured to have lower update time cost when clocked. When an inserter is not controlled by the circuit network (control behavior enabling / disabling it) it checks every craft finish of its source or sink machine if it has new items to pick up or new items to drop into the sink. Clocking can help by creating a window (active inserter window) narrowing the amount of ticks these checks can happen. Ideally, the inserter is always enabled "just in time" and can transfer in a batch as many items at once as it can.

## Wake-up Lists

Wake-up lists are how the game decides which idle inserters to check, and they are what goal 2 is measured in.

An inserter with nothing to move goes to sleep on the wake-up list of the machine it is waiting on. A machine has two lists and wakes them on different events: the inserters waiting to put items into it are woken when it starts a craft, and the inserters waiting to take items out of it when it finishes a craft or pays out a productivity bonus (both detailed below). A woken inserter looks for something to move, finds nothing or only part of a hand, and goes back to sleep. An inserter that is disabled by the circuit network is on no list and is never woken: the debug tooltip of a disabled inserter reads "Wakeup list count: 0".

This can be seen in the game with the debug setting `show-wakeup-lists`, which draws a yellow line from a waiting inserter to what it waits on. A line that flickers on and off very frequently is an inserter that keeps getting woken for nothing, and that is considered bad.

### Wake-ups, the measure for goal 2

A wake-up is one waking of an inserter by a machine while the inserter was enabled and asleep on its list. A machine wakes an inserter at most once in a tick. What wakes which list (factorio-benchmarks docs/mechanics, D7):
- An inserter waiting for space in a machine is woken when the machine starts a craft (consumes its ingredients), not when it pays out a productivity bonus. Confirmed on two designs, one machine type: the advanced circuit electromagnetic plant at +175% productivity, belt-fed inputs holding part of a hand, on the advanced circuit scaffold (252 of 252 starts and 0 of 210 lone payouts) and on the utility science baseline's dumps (0 of 22,548 lone payouts, 16,947 of 16,947 consumes, 0 of 94,292 ticks with no event; about 20 input histories) (factorio-benchmarks docs/mechanics, machines.md D7 at 50cf1626; its Rule 4's payout clause is superseded by D7 for inputs). Other machine types are not tested. It rests on reading the one-sample change of the input's status as a wake, on belt-fed inputs, and on the game's update order, which stay assumptions. The accumulator, low density structure and agricultural science recordings could not test it: on them no lone payout falls while an input waits for space with part of a hand. Whether it is the start or the finish of the craft before it is not known (D7 a2): on these builds they fall on the same tick. Within a build, a craft that finishes without consuming (at full output, with a payout) woke no input in 733 of 733 cases, against 16,947 of 16,947 consumes, which fits the wake coming with the consume; the mechanism itself stays open. An output taking the machine's products wakes a waiting part-hand belt input on every kind of tick (831 of 831, 820 of 820 and 3,863 of 3,863; one design; machines.md 4a at 50cf1626).
- An output waiting for items in a machine is released by a finished craft and by a productivity payout. Confirmed on three builds, outputs holding part of a hand only: 864 of 864 lone payouts on the advanced circuit, 210 of 210 on the batteries and 63 of 63 on the low density structures (D7 b), and reproduced on a fourth, agricultural science. An output with an empty hand is not known.
- Also open: whether a waiting inserter is also on its other machine's list is unconfirmed, and the simulator shows part-hand outputs waiting on their machine where the game shows them working.

The measure for goal 2 is the number of wake-ups per minute, summed over the inserters of one copy of the build. Among the clocks that meet goal 1, the one with the fewest wake-ups is the best. It is defined in `clock-generator/src/crafting/wake-ups.ts`. Since 0.13.0 it follows the mechanic above: the inserters into a machine are woken by its craft starts, which the simulator counts itself, and not by its payouts. Before that both lists were woken by finishes and payouts, which on a machine with productivity counted more wake-ups of the inserters into it than the game makes (the advanced circuit scaffold's inputs: 1,075 a minute against 625). The game's figure in a recording is read by the series' method from status and products_finished ("The rule's count and the clock-or-unwired advice"), whose standing per machine type is named with it; on a biochamber it is a known miss, and on machine types never recorded it is not checked.

Mining drills are not in the measure. A drill into a chest is still clocked (since 0.20.0, [drills.md](drills.md#a-drill-into-a-chest-on-a-clock)): an always-on drill is active every tick, and each tick it is disabled saves its update (given 2026-10-22). Its windows are made after the inserters' and can change their wake-ups only through what the inserters then do; on the steel wagon's selected clock they go from 81.6 to 84.1 a minute, which the drill's 885 disabled ticks a period are worth.

There are two kinds of design: a clock that only works when its inserters pick up from a backed-up belt, and a clock that also works when the items on the belt are moving. An inserter changes its tick timing when the belt is moving, and all the more when the belt has gaps, which it can have when the build needs the whole capacity of the belt. The simulator times reachable stacks on moving input lanes. Omitted `pickup_length` defaults to one tile; half-tile underground reach is explicit. Only `overrides.backed_up_belts: true` selects the backed-up pickup rule, independently of geometry. Most scaffolds have their belts closed at the end, so their input supply backs up once full. The iron bacteria scaffold lets 32 items through in every 61 ticks to keep its nutrients fresh, so its belt creeps. The electromagnetic science scaffolds under `tests/game/mechanics/belt-pickup/fixtures/different-items` drain the ingredient belt through a loader to keep supply moving.

What a build does when its belt moves has to be accounted for (decided on 2026-10-10). Recorded on 2026-10-17 on the three moving electromagnetic science scaffolds: the same default moving-belt clock, with 4 ticks of pickup slack, delivered 528 packs in all 35 periods on the straight belt, but fell short in 30 of 35 on each underground endpoint (480 to 528 packs, 28.779 a second against the target of 30). Isolated probes found slower grabs at either underground endpoint with moving supply, and consecutive grabs again with backed-up supply. The measurements and their limits are in [Picking up from moving belts and underground endpoints](simulator.md#picking-up-from-moving-belts-and-underground-endpoints). With the reachable-stack model, the new default delivered 352 packs per 704 ticks in all 52 measured periods on each scaffold. `moving-pickup.game.test.ts` checks throughput and recovery; supply through a row of copies remains outside the model.

Belt pickup slack adds margin for late pickups to the moving-stack timing model. Each window of an inserter that picks up from a belt stays open a few ticks longer: the ticks one more full hand takes at one belt stack a tick, which is 4 for a hand of 16 from stacks of 4. It is a rule of thumb. It may work on one build and not on another; the old always-full pickup model failed on the underground recordings above despite the slack. On moving supply, reachable stacks are modelled before the slack is added. A moving scaffold can expose that failure, but does not establish recovery by itself.

The generator selects the clock with the fewest wake-ups among those that meet goal 1, with one exception: it must not give up belt pickup slack to get there on its own. A clock without the slack is a clock for a backed-up belt, a backed-up belt clock, and in the simulator it looks better than it is on a moving belt. Leaving the slack out is a choice for the user, made with `overrides.backed_up_belts` in the config (the Belts section's "Backed up" answer in the UI): then each clock is made without the slack and the clock with the fewest wake-ups is selected; a clock that does not hold the target rate without the slack is offered as unstable, and is not made again with it. Without that choice a clock without the slack is only selected when no clock with it meets goal 1, whether the slack was left out on purpose or because the clock does not hold with it. The order is in `combineClockAlternativeRuns` in `clock-generator/src/crafting/generate-blueprint.ts`.

When no clock meets goal 1, the generator selects the one whose rate in the simulator is nearest to the target, above or below, whatever belt it is made for, and says so when its blueprint is copied. When no clock meets goal 1 and two are equally near the target rate, the one that holds its expected output from the generator's start is selected before one that does not: a clock that only fails to get back from a loss is nearer to goal 1 than one that drifts from a clean start. The processing unit sample with a belt export and the utility science sample with direct-insert low density structures are the two without a clock that holds: the first selects 31.8 of 40 a second (34.6 before the rules of issue 152 of 2026-10-14), the second 200 of 240.

The generator does not make every clock for every build (decided on 2026-10-12). Four of its clocks are found by a search, which tries many clocks in the simulator to find one: observed windows, uneven output swings, shifted swings and full-hand output swings. The others are made straight from the plan: batched swings, fractional swings, even hands and the other output swing counts. The searches take about nine tenths of the ticks a generation simulates, and they only run when no clock made without one can be selected as stable: one that holds its expected output, survives backpressure loss, meets the target rate and is made for the belt the config asks for. That is the test the selection itself puts first (`selectableAsStable` in `clock-generator/src/crafting/rank.ts`), so on a build with a deadlock risk a clock that holds at the target rate is enough, as it is for the selection. When the searches are needed all four run, and the selection is made among all the clocks as above.

So goal 1 decides whether the generator searches, and goal 2 does not. Where a clock made without a search is stable, a search could still find a stable clock with fewer wake-ups, and by default it is not looked for. Looking for it is a choice for the user, made with `overrides.search_for_fewer_wake_ups` in the config ("Search for fewer wake-ups" in the UI): then the searches run for every build and the clock with the fewest wake-ups of all is selected. Without it the generator names the searches it left out.


On the samples a clock made without a search is stable on 30 of 38 with belts moving and on 31 with backed-up belts, and there the searches are left out. That is 68% and 73% of the ticks simulated over all the samples, and 78% and 81% without the processing unit sample with a belt export, which takes more than a third of the time of them all and still searches. A search is the only source of a stable clock on four samples: automation science, advanced circuit at 230 a second, and logistic science with direct insertion and with inserter crafting. The selection changes on 7 samples with belts moving and on 8 with backed-up belts, each time from one stable clock to another; no sample loses a stable clock. The wake-ups given up are 0 to 43 a minute on all but two: 75 on the utility science chain (`UTILITY_SCIENCE_DIRECT_INSERT_LDS_CHAIN_ALWAYS_ON`, 787 to 862) and 90 on chemical science with engines with backed-up belts (`CHEMICAL_SCIENCE_ENGINES`, 255 to 345).

An inserter that takes fuel for its machine off a belt, as well as an ingredient, needs longer windows on a backed-up belt too. The fuel slot asks for a hand when it falls below its limit, which does not repeat with the clock: the hand of fuel comes at any tick of any window, ahead of the hands the window is planned for. A window ends where its last planned hand is back from the drop, so with a hand of fuel ahead that hand is full later than the window ends, by the ticks the fuel hand takes to fill: 4 for a hand of 16 from stacks of 4. Without them the window closes on a partly filled hand, and an inserter that holds an ingredient takes no fuel. This is room for a hand of fuel, and it is as long as belt pickup slack. A clock for backed-up belts gets it when it does not hold the target rate without, and stays a clock for a backed-up belt; one that does not hold with the room either is offered as it is, unstable. The agricultural science sample is the case: 377 of 400 a period in the game without the room, 400 with it. On a moving belt the two are not added up: the slack is all such an inserter has there, a hand of fuel uses it up, and nothing is left for a pickup that comes late. That is a known limit of the rule of thumb, left as it is so that the clocks for a moving belt stay as they are.

Every clock the generator offers names the belt it is made for: moving, with the ticks of slack it has, or backed-up. The slack is not given to a clock with a period that is not a whole number of ticks, so a subtick clock is a backed-up belt clock for now. It is not ranked behind the clocks for a moving belt, as a clock that left the slack out is. On the three samples with a subtick clock, no clock with the slack meets goal 1.

The inserters of a loop of machines are left always enabled by every clock, whatever that costs in wake-ups: a loop that runs dry does not start again. An inserter that takes from a machine of the loop to a machine outside it gets windows where the clock holds and gets back from every loss with them, and is left always enabled where it does not (since 0.23.0; before, only those into the machine the output is taken from could get windows, from the start of an output window to where that machine's output was back at its block, and only where the planning run held, which on agricultural science it never does: its loop runs free there and makes 432 of 400 a period). The machine it puts into takes nothing while its output is at its output block, and where that output is taken on a clock the stretches under the block are the same every period. So its windows are read from the clock's long run: in each stretch in which that machine was under its block, from the first run of ticks under it as long as the window, for as many ticks as the machine needs to draw the hands the stretch brings under its insertion limit ((items brought a stretch, less the limit and one) divided by what it draws a tick), and the hand's pickup, swing and drop, with 2 ticks on each side (`loopInserterWindows` in `clock-generator/src/crafting/candidates/loop-windows.ts`). The clock is checked with them over twice the usual long run, and they are kept only where it passes as it did without; where it then does not get back from a loss, the clock is made again without them. On agricultural science the two inserters that bring eggs to the science biochamber get 24 ticks of every 96 (its output is under its block from tick 25 of each output window; 32 eggs a stretch, a third of an egg drawn a tick, 27 under the limit: 15 ticks, and 5 for the hand), and the selected clock has 1,258 wake-ups a minute against 2,674 with them always enabled. The inserters between the egg machines were tried with windows too (2026-10-24): read in the same way from where their machines were under their block, they held and got back from every loss on some runs of the clock, 48 to 79 a minute fewer, and not on another, since the loop's own inserters empty those machines at times that do not repeat with the clock; narrower windows let the loop run dry at the start, where the seed runs out and every egg goes to the science biochamber. On the iron bacteria sample a clock with windows on its two loop inserters held the target rate in the game for ten minutes at 1,587 wake-ups a minute, where the clock selected then had 4,490; it is not offered.

Every clock says why it leaves an inserter or a drill always on, with no wake window (`always_on_reasons` of the result, named in the clock's inserter list): the build sets its enable rule to Always on; it is an inserter of a loop of machines, or takes from one where no window for it held; its swings fill the whole clock period, so a window would only put gaps between swings that follow each other (the hands in groups); it is a drill into a chest that nothing can time (`docs/drills.md`); or the run its windows are read from never swung it, so there is nothing to read a window from. A test generates every sample and fails on an inserter or drill that is on with no reason.

The game does not expose its wake-up lists to mods, so they cannot be recorded directly. They are worked out from what the game does show: the status of each inserter, which names what it is waiting for, and the number of products each machine has finished. The same calculation is used on a simulated run and on a recording of the game, so that the two can be compared.

Which list an inserter sleeps on follows its status: the machine it takes from while it is waiting for source items or for more items, and the machine it puts into while it is waiting for space in the destination. Whether a waiting inserter is also on the list of the machine at its other end has not been confirmed yet.

A clock can settle into more than one steady state with the same output, depending on what the machines hold when it starts, and each has wake-ups of its own. Batched swings on the utility science sample makes 192 a period in each of three, at 10.7, 26.8 and 32.1 wake-ups a minute; in the game it settled into the second. The generator counts the wake-ups of the state its own run settles into, so its figure for such a clock is one of several. A scaffold test looks for the game's figure among those the simulator counts from other starts of the clock.

Most clocks are like that. Surveyed on 2026-10-11 over the samples, from 24 places in the period to start each clock at (`clock-generator/.scratch/probes/steady-states/` on the machine the survey ran on): with belts moving, 91 of the 149 clocks whose long run counts wake-ups settle into more than one steady state, and 69 of 121 with backed-up belts. Batched swings on the gleba rocket fuel sample (`GLEBA_ROCKET_FUEL`) counts 1,187 a minute from the start the generator gives it and from 952 to 2,052 from the others, a different figure from each of the 24 starts. Fractional swings on the electric furnace sample (`ELECTRIC_FURNACE`) counts 33 from its own start and up to 96 from another. Batched swings without the slack on the flying robot frame sample (`FLYING_ROBOT_FRAME`) counts 12.5 from its own start and none from another. Over all the clocks surveyed, the range between the lowest and the highest figure is a third of the highest at the median, and the whole of it at the widest. The wake-ups the generator reports for a clock are the count from one start, its own, and that is what the selection compares. Ranked by the highest figure instead, 7 samples with belts moving and 8 without would select another clock; on utility science (`UTILITY_SCIENCE`) that would be the uneven output swings that deadlocked in the game (21.4 a minute from every start), over batched swings (10.7 from its own start, 32.1 at the highest), since on a build with a deadlock risk the wake-ups decide. Counting the range for the clocks that compete would cost about 60% more time searching, which is not spent (Working Rules, 6).

Belts and chests are left out of this measure. An inserter that works with a moving belt tracks the position of the item it is going to pick up, which costs more than a wake-up. Backed-up supply avoids that tracking in the game. The simulator models pickup timing for both supply settings; it does not assign a separate UPS cost to belt tracking.

### Even hands

A build in which one machine feeds several, or which is two halves doing the same thing, is best clocked so that every source is emptied by its takers in a fixed order, the same every period, and the two halves open and close together on one window decider for each thing they do. The batched swings plan does not give that: its windows are read off a planning run in which every inserter waits on its machines, so a machine with several takers gets whatever windows those waits made, and every cycle gets the same windows, which cannot move a hand count that is not a whole number a cycle.

Even hands (2026-10-14, for the accumulator build of issue 152) is made from the plan's hand counts and the rules of the game alone. Every inserter gets one window for each hand the plan has it move in the period, spread evenly over the period, each a tick shorter than its hand cycle. A window that long starts one hand and no more (pickups in a window = floor((W - 1) / cycle) + 1, factorio-benchmarks docs/mechanics, inserters.md section 9), at whichever of its ticks the source has the hand and the sink takes it. Inserters that move the same hands a period get the same windows, so the halves share deciders and two takers of one machine at one rate take from it in the same ticks. It is only made where every inserter takes from a machine or a chest (belt pickups fill over several ticks, and these windows are only derived for machine and chest sources), every inserter moves a whole number of hands a period, and no inserter is outside the plan (loops, fuel, by-products, drills). It is checked as every clock is, and is not a search.

The windows have to be that long. On the accumulator build windows of one tick at the same places made 144 of 288 a period: the plants run at 90 and 95% of their speed, a hand now and then finds 15 where it needs 16, takes a part hand and finishes it a window later, and an exact clock never makes that up. Windows of 7 held from 60 of 60 random places of the five groups in the period.

On the samples it is made for the accumulator build and the processing unit build (unstable there, 9.8 of 10 a second). On the accumulator build it is the only stable clock made without a search, and is selected, so the searches are left out there too (30 of 41 samples with belts moving and 32 of 41 with backed-up belts, on the full sweep of that day, against 29 and 31 before): 601.9 wake-ups a minute against 2,906.7 for the observed windows clock selected before, with 5 window deciders. Recorded on the scaffold: 576 in each of 46 periods, no difference between the simulator and the game, at 972 wake-ups a minute in the game, a figure the simulator counts (940.7) from another start of the clock (278.5 to 940.7 by where it starts).

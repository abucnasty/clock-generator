# Wake-ups and wake-up lists

This page was split out of GOALS.md on 2026-10-15; GOALS.md holds the goals.

## How clocking impacts updates per second
Clocking can reduce updates per second in theory by minimizing active inserter windows. Most notably, minimize the amount of crafts an inserter sees on its source or sink while idle and not transferring materials. These count as wasted updated checks. This doesn't always translate to improved performance on all hardware, but for the intents and purposes of this algorithm, it is assumed that it *always* does. The choice of removing a clock from an inserter is left up to the user and they can do this by overriding the inserter to "always" enabled in the config. An inserter that is set to "always" enabled is to get no combinator in the blueprint.

Given a recipe chain to produce a target end product, the amount of items that are needed are consistent in this build. Mathematically, the amount of items that need to be transferred is an independent concern of clocking. What clocking can control however is *when* those items get transferred.

Inserters have been measured to have lower update time cost when clocked. When an inserter is not controlled by the circuit network (control behavior enabling / disabling it) it checks every craft finish of its source or sink machine if it has new items to pick up or new items to drop into the sink. Clocking can help by creating a window (active inserter window) narrowing the amount of ticks these checks can happen. Ideally, the inserter is always enabled "just in time" and can transfer in a batch as many items at once as it can.

## Wake-up Lists

Wake-up lists are how the game decides which idle inserters to check, and they are what goal 2 is measured in.

An inserter with nothing to move goes to sleep on the wake-up list of the machine it is waiting on. A machine has two lists and wakes them on different events: the inserters waiting to put items into it are woken when it starts a craft, and the inserters waiting to take items out of it when it finishes a craft or pays out a productivity bonus (both detailed below). A woken inserter looks for something to move, finds nothing or only part of a hand, and goes back to sleep. An inserter that has been updated while disabled by the circuit network is on no list and is not woken: the debug tooltip of such an inserter reads "Wakeup list count: 0". An inserter that is switched off while it is asleep is not taken off its list (below, "Observed in the game at a switch-off").

This can be seen in the game with the debug setting `show-wakeup-lists`, which draws a yellow line from a waiting inserter to what it waits on. A line that flickers on and off very frequently is an inserter that keeps getting woken for nothing, and that is considered bad.

### Wake-ups, the measure for goal 2

A wake-up is one waking of an inserter by a machine while the inserter was enabled and asleep on its list. A machine wakes an inserter at most once in a tick. What wakes which list (factorio-benchmarks docs/mechanics, D7):
- An inserter waiting for space in a machine is woken when the machine starts a craft (consumes its ingredients), not when it pays out a productivity bonus. Confirmed on two designs, one machine type: the advanced circuit electromagnetic plant at +175% productivity, belt-fed inputs holding part of a hand, on the advanced circuit scaffold (252 of 252 starts and 0 of 210 lone payouts) and on the utility science baseline's dumps (0 of 22,548 lone payouts, 16,947 of 16,947 consumes, 0 of 94,292 ticks with no event; about 20 input histories) (factorio-benchmarks docs/mechanics, machines.md D7 at 50cf1626; its Rule 4's payout clause is superseded by D7 for inputs). Other machine types are not tested. It rests on reading the one-sample change of the input's status as a wake, on belt-fed inputs, and on the game's update order, which stay assumptions. The accumulator, low density structure and agricultural science recordings could not test it: on them no lone payout falls while an input waits for space with part of a hand. Whether it is the start or the finish of the craft before it is not known (D7 a2): on these builds they fall on the same tick. Within a build, a craft that finishes without consuming (at full output, with a payout) woke no input in 733 of 733 cases, against 16,947 of 16,947 consumes, which fits the wake coming with the consume; the mechanism itself stays open. An output taking the machine's products wakes a waiting part-hand belt input on every kind of tick (831 of 831, 820 of 820 and 3,863 of 3,863; one design; machines.md 4a at 50cf1626).
- An output waiting for items in a machine is released by a finished craft and by a productivity payout. Confirmed on three builds, outputs holding part of a hand only: 864 of 864 lone payouts on the advanced circuit, 210 of 210 on the batteries and 63 of 63 on the low density structures (D7 b), and reproduced on a fourth, agricultural science. An output with an empty hand is not known.
- An inserter waiting for the results of a machine that places them itself (a recycler), at its output or at the container it places them into, is woken in each tick it places a result there, of whatever item, and not by a craft that gives none: on scrap a stack inserter waiting with a part hand showed working in each of 1,708 ticks a result of another item came, and in none of 1,198 in which a craft gave nothing (one machine type, part hands). The simulator counts its placings, and the inserters that take from its result target sleep on its list. On the ore voider's cells the game woke the two inserters at the car 355 times a minute at exact speed and 286 overbeaconed, against 403 and 554 in the simulator; the difference is not explained yet and changes no selection (the other clock offered counts 852 and 1,385).
- An inserter whose arm is still swinging back from a drop that emptied its hand is on no list, whatever state the game shows: it shows "waiting for space" on the return swing when the machine it dropped into is full (the circuit export build's takers read W W W W F F F W, the F on the turn back). Since 0.26.0 the count takes the arm as away from the tick its hand empties for the turn's ticks and one (4 for the legendary stack inserter, ceil(0.5 / rotation speed) - 1), read off the hand, and keeps those alarms apart (`uncounted_per_minute`, "arm away"). On the simulator's runs this is exactly its own phase: an empty hand in the drop or the turn back. On the circuit export build's recommended clock it is 3,994 of 11,561 a minute, nearly all on the eight takers of the electronic circuit plants.
- Read on agricultural science (Factorio 2.1.24, below): an inserter waiting to put into a machine is also woken when the machine's burner takes a fuel item and when another inserter takes from the machine's output, at any output level, not only at its block; an inserter waiting for a machine's results is also woken when another inserter puts fuel into it. A fuel take did not wake an inserter waiting for the machine's results.
- A taker that took part of a hand sleeps on two lists (below, "Observed in the game at a switch-off"). The simulator shows an inserter on the update list with items it has not begun to move as working, as the game does (25 of 25 such ticks on the two captures).

#### Observed in the game at a switch-off, in one tick, and on two lists

Read in the running game (Factorio 2.1.24) on three builds, one capture each, 10 window cycles of the clock each (a fourth, metallurgic science, below): a front-exit build (one inserter takes from a machine onto a belt, one puts into it; window cycle time 1,408 ticks) and electromagnetic science (one inserter from a belt into the plant, two takers from the plant into a chest each; window cycle time 704 ticks), every window cycle the same, and agricultural science (five biochambers, twelve inserters, the egg machines burning the nutrients they are fed; window cycle time 480 ticks, from a staged start, its loop of eggs not the same each window cycle). The rule written from them gives the game's wake-ups tick for tick on the first two, and on agricultural science all 2,792 of them and 7 more (below). Standing: observed on those three builds, nothing wider. Not tested: an inserter with an empty hand at a belt drop without room.

- **A clock's switch-off sends no alarm.** An inserter that is in sleep on a machine's list when its clock switches it off stays on that list, on the input side or the output side, with an empty hand or a part hand. The machine's next alarm wakes it (a wakeup); in its next update it sees it is disabled and sleeps on no list. On agricultural science every inserter switched off in sleep kept its list until an alarm, on input lists and output lists alike, and no list was ever left without an alarm or a sleep; an alarm of any kind (a fuel take, an output taken, a craft start, a result) wakes it while it is off, so the next one finds it on no list. A disabled inserter carrying a full hand drops it and leaves the update list once its arm is back. On the front-exit build this is the output inserter at clock positions 288, 687 and 1072; on electromagnetic science the input inserter at 600 (the plant's craft start) and a taker at 460 (the plant's result, empty hand). A switch-on does alarm the inserter, and those are wakeups too (19 a window cycle on the front-exit build, 31 on electromagnetic science), which the count leaves out on purpose: they are the clock's doing, not a machine's.
- **An inserter can sleep during its own update and be woken again later in the same tick.** An inserter woken in one tick is updated in the next. If its hand is not full it goes back to sleep in that update, and a result the machine places later in the same tick wakes it again. This was seen with a part hand (front-exit, positions 24 and 280) and with an empty hand when the other taker, updated first, had taken the whole output of the machine (electromagnetic science, positions 76 and 267).
- **A taker that took part of a hand sleeps on two lists:** its machine's output list and the list of what it puts into (electromagnetic science: 10 of 10 and 8 of 11 such sleeps into a chest; the other 3 had an empty hand and slept on the machine's list alone). Of the front-exit build's output inserter it was 80 of 150 sleeps, the second list not the machine's. A chest's or a belt's list never woke an inserter in the captures. A machine's does: on agricultural science a taker from one biochamber into another slept on the source's output list and the sink's input list after a part take (212 + 6 of 218 such sleeps), and with an empty hand while the source held items the sink did not take (1,814); with an empty hand at an empty source on the source's list alone, and where the sink took nothing at all on the sink's list alone. The sink's craft starts, fuel takes and outputs taken woke the egg takers into the science biochamber there 24.5 times a window cycle. An inserter in sleep on two lists is woken by whichever alarms first. An alarm to an inserter not in sleep changes nothing: every alarm that did not wake an inserter in the captures reached one not in sleep.
- **What an inserter waits for is decided in its update.** The state the game shows after a tick is read after the machines have done their part of it, so a result placed later in the tick can turn "waiting for source items" into "waiting for space in the destination" for an inserter that went to sleep waiting for items. The game also shows "waiting for source items" in the tick a clock switches an inserter on, while it is on the update list.
- **A hand from a belt with nothing it may take sleeps only while the belt is still.** On metallurgic science (four machines, eight inserters, window cycle time 640 ticks, ten settled window cycles) two inserters take ore from the near lane of a backed-up turbo belt of stacks of four, fed from its start, into an assembler that holds its insertion limit of the ore, and a third takes from the far lane into a foundry. Shown waiting for source items with an empty hand, the two ask to sleep in every update at the pickup, and the game lets them only while both lines of the belt are still (56 of 56 sleeps each with both still; of 1,216 refusals each, 1,210 with a line moving and 6 in a tick the third took from the belt, which depend on the order of updates). They then sleep on the assembler's input list and on both lines' lists, and its craft starts (54) and an output taken (2) woke them, 5.6 times a window cycle each. A line moves while it closes the gaps that takes leave: a take of a whole stack leaves a gap of a stack, and the line moves from that tick on, a stack in 2 ticks on this belt, and stops in the first tick no gap is left (17 ticks after the two take a hand of four stacks each side by side, 3 to 9 after the third takes one to four stacks). Worked out from the takes in the recording, that gives every tick the game moved either line, 9,658 of 9,658, and no other. A take that set a line moving did not wake the inserters in sleep on its list (2 of 2); what wakes a line's list was not seen here. An inserter whose machine takes nothing at all (its output at the block) shows waiting for space and sleeps from the drop without reading the lines (the third inserter). Not tested: other belt speeds and stack sizes, a belt that is not backed up or has a taker downstream, other pickups than the side of a straight belt, a part hand.
- **Who is updated first.** The game updates first the inserter that joined the update list last: one woken by another inserter's take, then one woken by a machine, then one switched on by its clock, in that order within a tick. So a take by an inserter that joined the list earlier comes after the update of one that joined later, and wakes it again if it went to sleep in that update. Two switched on in one tick, and two woken by two machines in one tick, come in an order a recording does not hold: the rule takes the other first in the first case (81 of 81 on agricultural science) and this one first in the second, which the game did there in some ticks and not in others: the 7 extra wake-ups it counts on agricultural science are of this kind.

What the count does with this. The count follows these (the rule "kept-lists" in `src/crafting/wake-ups.ts`), with the simulator's update list (`src/simulation/dispatch.ts`), the only dispatch since 0.27.0. Before it the count read each tick an inserter is shown disabled as a tick on no list, so the switch-offs above and the same-tick wakings were not counted (on the front-exit build 10 wakeups a window cycle where the game has 15 by a machine, on electromagnetic science 11 where the game has 31). It works out the lists of each inserter tick by tick from the start of a run, both lists of a taker between two machines included, and on a recording of the game reads each machine's craft starts off its ingredients and its burner's fuel takes off the fuel it has left. The simulator's own lists follow the same observations: a burner's fuel take and a hand putting fuel in alarm the machine's lists, so a hand that carries fuel sleeps instead of being updated every tick. On agricultural science from the capture's start its own wakeups are within 3% of the game's per inserter and at the game's ticks for 97% of them; the rest are fuel taken a tick early, after the first stop at a full output the simulator's burning item runs a fraction of a tick ahead of the game's. A belt line in the simulator moves as above, and an empty hand from the side of a straight belt, shown waiting for source items, sleeps only while its lines are still; the rule works the lines out from the takes of the inserters on the same belt. On metallurgic science, started from the game's machines and hands at the first counted tick, the two belt-fed inserters' own wakeups fall at the game's ticks, all of them and no other, and the rule gives the game's wakeups on its recording tick for tick on every inserter but four of the foundry feeder's (in sleep with a part hand from its switch-off, which the rule does not read).

#### Enable windows shaped against ghost wakeups

A ghost wakeup costs a wakeup and does nothing, and the clock decides whether it happens: it comes only where an enable window's closing edge finds its inserter in sleep on a machine's list. Since 0.28.0 (#641) the generator shapes the windows of the primary clock of batched swings (the planned clock, or the one by arithmetic or with the belt slack inside its windows that takes its place) against them. It reads the clock-only run of that clock: for each ghost wakeup, the window that closed last before it, the tick the inserter went to sleep, and the tick it took the hand it held then, if any. The window then closes so that the inserter's update in that tick is its first disabled one: it finds its hand empty and its arm at home, and goes on no list. A window that this leaves with nothing is dropped. Every window is shaped at once and checked as the clock is (the clock-only check from every start phase and the long run); where that does not hold or wake fewer, the windows of one inserter at a time. The shaped clock takes the primary's place where it holds, wakes its inserters fewer times a minute and gets back from every loss the primary gets back from (`withoutGhostWakeUps` in `src/crafting/generate-clock.ts`, `src/crafting/plan/ghost-windows.ts`).

Moving the closing edge is the clean case, and the only one made. On the electric furnace sample the output inserter took a part hand of 6 at the end of each of its 9 windows a window cycle and slept with it until the next result came while it was off (9 ghost wakeups a window cycle, as many in the game): closed 8 ticks earlier, the windows leave the 6 for the next window, and the clock wakes 62.5 times a minute against 100. Not shaped: the windows of an inserter that picks up from a moving belt (its belt slack), of a loop's inserters, of grouped hands and of the clocks of other makers. Where the hand at the end of a window is one the build needs, the earlier closing edge does not hold: on the logistic science sample the output inserter's last part hand of 10 keeps the assembler from blocking at its output, and its ghost wakeup, all of its 20.57 wakeups a minute, stays. R15 (0.27.0) measured the same on two other builds: a closing-edge move alone cleared 1 of 3 and 2 of 6 ghost-causing windows; the rest needed the window dropped or changed otherwise.

The measure for goal 2 is the number of wake-ups per minute, summed over the inserters of one copy of the build. Among the clocks that meet goal 1, the one with the fewest wake-ups is the best. It is defined in `clock-generator/src/crafting/wake-ups.ts`. Since 0.13.0 it follows the mechanic above: the inserters into a machine are woken by its craft starts, which the simulator counts itself, and not by its payouts (under the rule "kept-lists" also by its fuel takes and its output being taken, as above). Before that both lists were woken by finishes and payouts, which on a machine with productivity counted more wake-ups of the inserters into it than the game makes (the advanced circuit scaffold's inputs: 1,075 a minute against 625). The game's figure in a recording is read by the series' method from status and products_finished ("The rule's count and the clock-or-unwired advice"), whose standing per machine type is named with it; on a biochamber it is a known miss, and on machine types never recorded it is not checked.

#### Wake-ups from more than one start

A build on a fixed clock can settle into more than one steady state, and which one depends on how it starts: where its clock stands when its machines start empty, and, for a loop, what the loop holds. Each start settles for good: on the iron bacteria sample with one window a window cycle on its loop inserters the wake-ups from five starts stayed at 317, 373, 756, 766 and 907 a minute, each within 2% over ten stretches of 12,000 ticks of a run of 180,000. The places in the window cycle that settle alike form bands (there, the first 13 ticks all give 373.3).

So on a build with a loop of machines, or a machine that makes its own ingredient, which holds a stock its clock does not set, a clock that meets the setpoint is ranked by its mean wakeups over a fixed set of starts (decided 2026-10-24): the config's cold start with the clock at twelve evenly spaced places in its window cycle, the places the as-built check starts it from, the loop seeded as the config says (`overrides.kick_start_items`). The seed is not averaged over: it moves the mean (the iron bacteria clock above, over every place: 495 at a seed of 200, 566 at 100, 670 at 50), so it belongs to the build the config describes. Each start is run as the clock's own long run is and counted over whole window cycles (or whole repeats of a fractional window cycle) of its judged part, so that two starts that settle alike count the same. The clocks that do not meet the setpoint keep the cold figure, since their wakeups do not decide the selection. `clock-generator/src/crafting/wake-ups-from-starts.ts` has the set and the rule.

Every other build keeps the one figure of its cold start for now (decided 2026-10-24, until the simulator's wake-ups are nearer the game's). In the simulator 86 of the 247 stable clocks of the samples without a loop have a mean over the twelve places more than 1% off their cold figure (electric furnace 75 a minute from the cold start, 37.5 to 87.5 by place), and ranking them by it (with a probe of two places first) moved the selection on nine samples and a fixture, at 3.3% more generation time where no clock got a mean and 16.3% where some did; in the game the low density structure clock woke 581 to 656 a minute from three places where the simulator counted 656 to 881.

Twelve is not a sample that is exact. Against every place of the window cycle, twelve evenly spaced ones were within 7% of the mean on 22 loop clocks and seeds (iron bacteria and agricultural science with windows on their loops, and variants; median 1.6%), within 1% on 10 of them; twenty-four were within 3.6% (median 0.4%). A set of 15 starts (the cold start, seven seeds, seven places) cannot be matched to 1% by fewer of them (14 of the 15 were within 1% on 39% of 1,636 clocks, the worst 25% off), and its own mean moved 5% to 25% against 15 other starts. Twelve are the places the as-built check already judges a clock from.

Mining drills are not in the measure. A drill into a chest is still clocked (since 0.20.0, [drills.md](drills.md#a-drill-into-a-chest-on-a-clock)): an always-on drill is on the update list every tick, and each tick it is disabled saves its update (given 2026-10-22). Its windows are made after the inserters' and can change their wakeups only through what the inserters then do; on the steel wagon's recommended clock they go from 81.6 to 84.1 a minute, which the drill's 885 disabled ticks a window cycle are worth.

There are two kinds of design: a clock that only works when its inserters pick up from a backed-up belt, and a clock that also works when the items on the belt are moving. An inserter changes its tick timing when the belt is moving, and all the more when the belt has gaps, which it can have when the build needs the whole capacity of the belt. The simulator times reachable stacks on moving input lanes. Omitted `pickup_length` defaults to one tile; half-tile underground reach is explicit. Only `overrides.backed_up_belts: true` selects the backed-up pickup rule, independently of geometry. Most scaffolds have their belts closed at the end, so their input supply backs up once full. The iron bacteria scaffold lets 32 items through in every 61 ticks to keep its nutrients fresh, so its belt creeps. The electromagnetic science scaffolds under `tests/game/mechanics/belt-pickup/fixtures/different-items` drain the ingredient belt through a loader to keep supply moving.

What a build does when its belt moves has to be accounted for (decided on 2026-10-10). Recorded on 2026-10-17 on the three moving electromagnetic science scaffolds: the same default moving-belt clock, with 4 ticks of belt slack, delivered 528 packs in all 35 window cycles on the straight belt, but fell short in 30 of 35 on each underground endpoint (480 to 528 packs, 28.779 a second against the target of 30). Isolated probes found slower grabs at either underground endpoint with moving supply, and consecutive grabs again with backed-up supply. The measurements and their limits are in [Picking up from moving belts and underground endpoints](simulator.md#picking-up-from-moving-belts-and-underground-endpoints). With the reachable-stack model, the new default delivered 352 packs per 704 ticks in all 52 measured window cycles on each scaffold. `moving-pickup.game.test.ts` checks throughput and recovery; supply through a row of copies remains outside the model.

Belt pickup slack adds margin for late pickups to the moving-stack timing model. Each window of an inserter that picks up from a belt stays open a few ticks longer: the ticks one more full hand takes at one belt stack a tick, which is 4 for a hand of 16 from stacks of 4. It is a rule of thumb. It may work on one build and not on another; the old always-full pickup model failed on the underground recordings above despite the slack. On moving supply, reachable stacks are modelled before the slack is added. A moving scaffold can expose that failure, but does not establish recovery by itself.

A window read off the planning run holds part of that margin already: it closes a rotation before its last hand's arm is back at the belt, so it stays open the hand's rotation, its drop and a tick after the hand's last pickup (5 ticks for a legendary stack inserter). Where every belt-fed window of a clock stays open the slack's ticks after its last pickup, the slack is already inside the windows, and the ticks added after them only let the arm, back from its last drop, start a hand the plan does not have; where that hand has to wait at its machine, it costs a wakeup. Such a clock is made both ways, and the one with the slack inside its windows, none added, takes the other's place where it holds, wakes its inserters fewer times and gets back from every loss the other gets back from (since 0.28.0, #595). It is still a clock for a moving belt, labelled as one with the slack added ("moving (+4 ticks)"). On the logistic science sample the clock of 4 output swings in 176 ticks has two pickup windows, 52-71 and 76-95: with 4 ticks added after each they join into one (52-99) and cost 61.71 wakeups a minute; with the slack inside they cost 20.57, the price of the hand-made clock of the same shape (pickups 50-68 and 78-94, 20.45 a minute in the game), whose windows with 4 ticks added after each, still apart, cost 41.14.

The generator selects the clock with the fewest wakeups among those that meet goal 1, with one exception: it must not give up belt slack to get there on its own (a clock with the slack inside its windows has not given it up). A clock without the slack is a clock for a backed-up belt, a backed-up belt clock, and in the simulator it looks better than it is on a moving belt. Leaving the slack out is a choice for the user, made with `overrides.backed_up_belts` in the config (the Belts section's "Backed up" answer in the UI): then each clock is made without the slack and the clock with the fewest wakeups is selected; a clock that does not hold the setpoint without the slack is offered as unstable, and is not made again with it. Without that choice a clock without the slack is only selected when no clock with it meets goal 1, whether the slack was left out on purpose or because the clock does not hold with it. The order is in `combineClockAlternativeRuns` in `clock-generator/src/crafting/generate-blueprint.ts`.

When no clock meets goal 1, the generator selects a stable clock that runs over the target, the nearest of them, before any clock nearer to the target that is unstable, does not go back to its rate after a disturbance or runs under it (since 0.26.0: on Thaeln's cell 07 a stable clock 2.3% over is selected, not an unstable one 0.2% under); where there is none, the one whose rate in the simulator is nearest to the target, above or below, whatever belt it is made for, and it says so when its blueprint is copied. When no clock meets goal 1 and two are equally near the setpoint, the one that holds its expected output from the cold start is selected before one that does not: a clock that only fails to go back to its rate after a disturbance is nearer to goal 1 than one that drifts from a clean start. The processing unit sample with a belt export and the utility science sample with direct-insert low density structures (both since dropped from the samples) were the two without a clock that holds: the first selects 31.8 of 40 a second (34.6 before the rules of issue 152 of 2026-10-14), the second 200 of 240. The first has a window cycle of 116,160 ticks (348,480 with 3 output swings), and generating it took over five minutes (#101) while a typical sample takes about 2 s. 60% of a generation went into grouping its 70,000 or so transfers a call, which a sorted sweep now does (#406), and a run made again with the same config and options within a generation is now made once, as is a clock's loss verdict (#408, #409): on one desktop machine it generates in 68 s with belts moving and 62 s backed up, where it took 260 s and 231 s, and sharing the text of equal transition reasons (#433) lowers its peak memory from 3.10 to 2.65 GiB. The times are for comparing the versions with each other, not absolute.

The generator does not make every clock for every build (decided on 2026-10-12). Four of its clocks are found by a search, which tries many clocks in the simulator to find one: observed windows, uneven output swings, shifted swings and full-hand output swings. The others are made straight from the plan: batched swings, fractional swings, even hands and the other output swing counts. The searches take about nine tenths of the ticks a generation simulates, and they only run when no clock made without one can be selected as stable: one that holds its expected output, survives every disturbance trial, meets the setpoint and is made for the belt the config asks for. That is the test the selection itself puts first (`selectableAsStable` in `clock-generator/src/crafting/rank.ts`), so on a build with a deadlock risk a clock that holds at the setpoint is enough, as it is for the selection. When the searches are needed all four run, and the selection is made among all the clocks as above.

So goal 1 decides whether the generator searches, and goal 2 does not. Where a clock made without a search is stable, a search could still find a stable clock with fewer wake-ups. Since 0.26.0 the two searches that did so on the samples, observed windows and shifted swings, run past a stable clock that has wake-ups on a primary window cycle time of at most 480 ticks (`searchesToRun`, `rank.ts`; with every search run on every sample as built and backed up they found fewer wake-ups on 8 of 106 jobs, at 22.7% of the builds running all four everywhere costs); the other two are not looked for by default. Looking for it is a choice for the user, made with `overrides.search_for_fewer_wake_ups` in the config ("Search for fewer wake-ups" in the UI): then the searches run for every build and the clock with the fewest wake-ups of all is selected. Without it the generator names the searches it left out.


On the samples a clock made without a search is stable on 30 of 38 with belts moving and on 31 with backed-up belts, and there the searches are left out. That is 68% and 73% of the ticks simulated over all the samples, and 78% and 81% without the processing unit sample with a belt export, which takes more than a third of the time of them all and still searches. A search is the only source of a stable clock on four samples: automation science, advanced circuit at 230 a second, and logistic science with direct insertion and with inserter crafting. The selection changes on 7 samples with belts moving and on 8 with backed-up belts, each time from one stable clock to another; no sample loses a stable clock. The wake-ups given up are 0 to 43 a minute on all but two: 75 on the utility science chain (787 to 862; a sample since dropped) and 90 on chemical science with engines with backed-up belts (`CHEMICAL_SCIENCE_ENGINES`, 255 to 345).

An inserter that takes fuel for its machine off a belt, as well as an ingredient, needs longer windows on a backed-up belt too. The fuel slot asks for a hand when it falls below its limit, which does not repeat with the clock: the hand of fuel comes at any tick of any window, ahead of the hands the window is planned for. A window ends where its last planned hand is back from the drop, so with a hand of fuel ahead that hand is full later than the window ends, by the ticks the fuel hand takes to fill: 4 for a hand of 16 from stacks of 4. Without them the window closes on a partly filled hand, and an inserter that holds an ingredient takes no fuel. This is room for a hand of fuel, and it is as long as belt slack. A clock for backed-up belts gets it when it does not hold the setpoint without, and stays a clock for a backed-up belt; one that does not hold with the room either is offered as it is, unstable. The agricultural science sample is the case: 377 of 400 a window cycle in the game without the room, 400 with it. On a moving belt the two are not added up: the slack is all such an inserter has there, a hand of fuel uses it up, and nothing is left for a pickup that comes late. That is a known limit of the rule of thumb, left as it is so that the clocks for a moving belt stay as they are.

Every clock the generator offers names the belt it is made for: moving, with the ticks of slack it has, or backed-up. The slack is not given to a clock with a window cycle time that is not a whole number of ticks, so a subtick clock is a backed-up belt clock for now. It is not ranked behind the clocks for a moving belt, as a clock that left the slack out is. On the three samples with a subtick clock, no clock with the slack meets goal 1.

The inserters of a loop of machines are left always enabled by the plan, whatever that costs in wakeups: a loop that runs dry does not start again. A clock gives them windows placed by arithmetic where it holds and gets back from every loss with them (since 0.25.0, below). An inserter that takes from a machine of the loop to a machine outside it gets windows where the clock holds and gets back from every loss with them, and is left always enabled where it does not (since 0.23.0; before, only those into the machine the output is taken from could get windows, from the start of an output window to where that machine's output was back at its block, and only where the planning run held, which on agricultural science it never does: its loop runs free there and makes 432 of 400 a window cycle). The machine it puts into takes nothing while its output is at its output block, and where that output is taken on a clock the stretches under the block are the same every window cycle. So its windows are read from the clock's long run: in each stretch in which that machine was under its block, from the first run of ticks under it as long as the window, for as many ticks as the machine needs to draw the hands the stretch brings under its insertion limit ((items brought a stretch, less the limit and one) divided by what it draws a tick), and the hand's pickup, swing and drop, with 2 ticks on each side (`loopInserterWindows` in `clock-generator/src/crafting/candidates/loop-windows.ts`). The clock is checked with them over twice the usual long run, and they are kept only where it passes as it did without; where it then does not go back to its rate after a disturbance, the clock is made again without them. On agricultural science the two inserters that bring eggs to the science biochamber get 24 ticks of every 96 (its output is under its block from tick 25 of each output window; 32 eggs a stretch, a third of an egg drawn a tick, 27 under the limit: 15 ticks, and 5 for the hand), and the recommended clock has 1,258 wakeups a minute against 2,674 with them always enabled. The inserters between the egg machines were tried with windows too (2026-10-24): read in the same way from where their machines were under their block, they held and got back from every loss on some runs of the clock, 48 to 79 a minute fewer, and not on another, since the loop's own inserters empty those machines at times that do not repeat with the clock; narrower windows let the loop run dry at the start, where the seed runs out and every egg goes to the science biochamber. On the iron bacteria sample a clock with windows on its two loop inserters held the setpoint in the game for ten minutes at 1,587 wakeups a minute, where the clock selected then had 4,490.

The loop's own inserters get windows after those (since 0.25.0, `inLoopInserterWindows` in the same file), placed from the clock's own windows, never read from where a run filled a hand, by the kind of the machine each puts into. Where that machine takes nothing while its output is at an output block under a stack of its product, and takes the loop's item only under an insertion limit below a hand (the egg biochambers: block 14 under a stack of 20, limit 7 under a hand of 16), it takes a hand just after its output has been taken under the block, so the loop's inserters take turns after each window of that machine's takers out of the loop, or after each output window of the machine those put into where there are more of those: with n inserters in the loop and a slot of the window cycle over those windows, the j-th in the order of the build is on from a swing after each window opens, plus j slots / n, for slot / n + 2 ticks. Where those takers have no windows out of the loop (none held, or the window cycle is not a whole number of ticks) it stays always enabled. Where the machine's insertion limit holds a hand and its output block is not under a stack (the iron bacteria biochambers: limit 28, hand 16), a hand it refuses is taken at the next window before it runs dry, so the inserter gets one window of a swing and a tick for each hand it moved a window cycle in the clock's long run with the loop always enabled, spaced evenly from midway between the openings of the first two output windows of the machine it takes from. Any other inserter of a loop stays always enabled. They are kept only where the clock's check passes with them as it did without and still passes with each set of identical windows of the loop, those out of it included, moved a tick earlier or later (the as-built check from 12 start phases with its standard long run); where the clock then does not go back to its rate after a disturbance it is made again with windows only out of the loop, then with none. On agricultural science the two inserters between the egg biochambers get 50 ticks of every 96, 48 apart, and the recommended clock has 881 wakeups a minute from its cold start (867 on the mean over 12 start places) against 1,258 (1,246) with them always enabled; in the game, with the family's cuts, 400 in every window cycle before them and back from the second window cycle after each, 1,269 wakeups a minute against 1,712 on the clock before. On iron bacteria the two loop inserters get 9 ticks twice a window cycle of 90.95, 516 a minute from the cold start (814 on the mean) against 3,535 (3,607); in the game 10,944 in every wrap of 1,728 ticks and back from the first wrap after each cut, 1,233 a minute. Neither is the fewest a loop can have: a search over window placements (2026-10-24) found clocks with about 10% fewer on agricultural science and about 40% fewer on iron bacteria on the mean, which no rule by arithmetic matches yet; on iron bacteria the best placement of one window a window cycle moves from start to start, and the game does not follow the simulator's order of placements.

Every clock says why it leaves an inserter or a drill always on, with no enable window (`always_on_reasons` of the result, named in the clock's inserter list): the build sets its override to Always on; it is an inserter of a loop of machines, or takes from one where no window for it held; its swings fill the whole window cycle, so a window would only put gaps between swings that follow each other (the hands in groups); it is a drill into a chest that nothing can time (`docs/drills.md`); or the run its windows are read from never swung it, so there is nothing to read a window from. A test generates every sample and fails on an inserter or drill that is on with no reason.

The game does not expose its wake-up lists to mods, so they cannot be recorded directly. They are worked out from what the game does show: the status of each inserter, which names what it is waiting for, and the number of products each machine has finished. The same calculation is used on a simulated run and on a recording of the game, so that the two can be compared.

Which list an inserter sleeps on follows its status: the machine it takes from while it is waiting for source items or for more items, and the machine it puts into while it is waiting for space in the destination. A taker that took part of a hand is also on the list of what it puts into: a chest's did not wake it in the captures read in the game, a machine's does (above); an inserter that is switched off while asleep keeps its list until the next alarm.

A clock can settle into more than one steady state with the same output, depending on what the machines hold when it starts, and each has wake-ups of its own. Batched swings on the utility science sample makes 192 a window cycle in each of three, at 10.7, 26.8 and 32.1 wake-ups a minute; in the game it settled into the second. The generator counts the wake-ups of the state its own run settles into, so its figure for such a clock is one of several. A scaffold test looks for the game's figure among those the simulator counts from other starts of the clock.

Most clocks are like that. Surveyed on 2026-10-11 over the samples, from 24 places in the window cycle to start each clock at (`clock-generator/.scratch/probes/steady-states/` on the machine the survey ran on): with belts moving, 91 of the 149 clocks whose long run counts wake-ups settle into more than one steady state, and 69 of 121 with backed-up belts. Batched swings on the gleba rocket fuel sample (`ROCKET_FUEL_60`) counts 1,187 a minute from the start the generator gives it and from 952 to 2,052 from the others, a different figure from each of the 24 starts. Fractional swings on the electric furnace sample (`ELECTRIC_FURNACE_20`) counts 33 from its own start and up to 96 from another. Batched swings without the slack on the flying robot frame sample (`FLYING_ROBOT_FRAME_160`) counts 12.5 from its own start and none from another. Over all the clocks surveyed, the range between the lowest and the highest figure is a third of the highest at the median, and the whole of it at the widest. The wake-ups the generator reports for a clock are the count from one start, its own, and that is what the selection compares. Ranked by the highest figure instead, 7 samples with belts moving and 8 without would select another clock; on utility science (`UTILITY_SCIENCE_120`) that would be the uneven output swings that deadlocked in the game (21.4 a minute from every start), over batched swings (10.7 from its own start, 32.1 at the highest), since on a build with a deadlock risk the wake-ups decide. Counting the range for the clocks that compete would cost about 60% more time searching, which is not spent (Working Rules, 6).

Beside the wake-ups, each clock alternative carries per-inserter counters from the same run and the same ticks (`as_built.counters`, `src/crafting/inserter-counters.ts`), a minute like the wake-ups: each inserter's wake-ups split by the list that woke it (input: the machine it puts into; output: the machine it takes from), the kinds of its two ends, grabs, swings, part-hand swings and the grabs beyond one a swing, the ticks it works and waits (each wait keyed by the end it waits at and what is there), its switch-ons and switch-offs (a switch-on that finds the arm at rest or still moving), the inserters on its decider times the copies, and for a machine pickup the crafts that finish in the tick after a wake-up while the inserter is still at its pickup. Nothing selects, ranks or judges a clock by them yet: the ranking stays the wake-ups. The stack of a belt ahead of a pickup is not counted, since the stock records no series for a belt.

Also beside the wakeups, each clock carries the signals a minute its window deciders send (`assessment.signals_per_minute`, `src/crafting/clock-signals.ts`), from the same run and ticks. A decider sends one signal each time one of its enable windows opens, and that switches on every inserter wired to it, so inserters that share a decider share its signals. The count is of signals, not of inserters switched on: the most switch-ons of a decider's inserters, and for a decider of chest drills the windows the drills have in a window cycle. A window's closing edge is not counted, because each opening edge has one. A signal costs the game time on its own, for every inserter on the network, and how that weighs against a wakeup is not measured: nothing ranks a clock by it yet. It is null where the wakeups are.

Belts and chests are left out of this measure. An inserter that works with a moving belt tracks the position of the item it is going to pick up, which costs more than a wake-up. Backed-up supply avoids that tracking in the game. The simulator models pickup timing for both supply settings; it does not assign a separate UPS cost to belt tracking.

### Even hands

A build in which one machine feeds several, or which is two halves doing the same thing, is best clocked so that every source is emptied by its takers in a fixed order, the same every window cycle, and the two halves open and close together on one window decider for each thing they do. The batched swings plan does not give that: its windows are read off a planning run in which every inserter waits on its machines, so a machine with several takers gets whatever windows those waits made, and every cycle gets the same windows, which cannot move a hand count that is not a whole number a cycle.

Even hands (2026-10-14, for the accumulator build of issue 152) is made from the plan's hand counts and the rules of the game alone. Every inserter gets one window for each hand the plan has it move in the window cycle, spread evenly over the window cycle, each a tick shorter than its hand cycle. A window that long starts one hand and no more (pickups in a window = floor((W - 1) / cycle) + 1, factorio-benchmarks docs/mechanics, inserters.md section 9), at whichever of its ticks the source has the hand and the sink takes it. Inserters that move the same hands a window cycle get the same windows, so the halves share deciders and two takers of one machine at one rate take from it in the same ticks. It is only made where every inserter takes from a machine or a chest (belt pickups fill over several ticks, and these windows are only derived for machine and chest sources), every inserter moves a whole number of hands a window cycle, and no inserter is outside the plan (loops, fuel, by-products, drills). It is checked as every clock is, and is not a search.

The windows have to be that long. On the accumulator build windows of one tick at the same places made 144 of 288 a window cycle: the plants run at 90 and 95% of their speed, a hand now and then finds 15 where it needs 16, takes a part hand and finishes it a window later, and an exact clock never makes that up. Windows of 7 held from 60 of 60 random places of the five groups in the window cycle.

On the samples it is made for the accumulator build and the processing unit build (unstable there, 9.8 of 10 a second). On the accumulator build it is the only stable clock made without a search, and is selected, so the searches are left out there too (30 of 41 samples with belts moving and 32 of 41 with backed-up belts, on the full sweep of that day, against 29 and 31 before): 601.9 wake-ups a minute against 2,906.7 for the observed windows clock selected before, with 5 window deciders. Recorded on the scaffold: 576 in each of 46 window cycles, no difference between the simulator and the game, at 972 wake-ups a minute in the game, a figure the simulator counts (940.7) from another start of the clock (278.5 to 940.7 by where it starts).
